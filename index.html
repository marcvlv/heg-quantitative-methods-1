Part 0 · Levelling up, before the course starts

Why this part exists

The syllabus lists pre-algebra and pre-calculus as prerequisites. This part is that prerequisite, written out.

Quantitative Methods does not teach arithmetic, fractions, powers or how to solve an equation. It assumes them, from the first slide. Week 1 opens with "Levelling Up" and moves through first-order equations, systems and quadratics in nine slides, at a speed that only works if none of it is new.

So this part comes first. It assumes nothing. If you can already do all of it, you will lose twenty minutes confirming that, and the rest of the document will be easier. If you cannot, this is the part that decides your semester, because every later topic is built on it: a derivative is a limit of a fraction, an elasticity is a ratio of percentages, a present value is a power of (1 + r), and a regression line is y = mx + b with data attached.

On Cyberlearn. Week 1 of the course points to a Levelling Up module: videos, a quiz, and a blank HES-SO entry exam. The syllabus calls it an optional online levelling-up course. It is the official version of this part, it is free, and it is the fastest way to find out which of the sections below you actually need.
How to use Part 0
  • Read the rule, then cover the worked example and try it yourself.
  • Do the practice question at the end of each section on paper.
  • If a section is easy, skip to the next. If a section is hard, that is the one to repeat tomorrow.

Numbers, notation and the order of operations

The symbols the slides use from the first line, and the rule that decides every calculation.

The sets of numbers

The course writes things like "Let a, b ∈ ℝ, a ≠ 0" on slide 4 of week 1. That sentence is read: "let a and b be real numbers, with a not equal to zero". Four sets appear.

One rule, applied left to right within each level

1. Brackets. 2. Powers and roots. 3. Multiplication and division. 4. Addition and subtraction.

The minus sign and powers

−32 = −9, because the power binds tighter than the minus. (−3)2 = 9, because the bracket makes the minus part of the base. Your calculator obeys this too, and it is the single most common reason a calculator "disagrees" with you.

What to remember
  • ℝ is the world this course lives in; ℝ\{a} means "every real number except a".
  • Square brackets include the endpoint, reversed brackets exclude it.
  • Brackets, powers, then × and ÷, then + and −.

Half of the algebra errors in this course are fraction errors, and they never announce themselves.

Dividing by a fraction: why you invert

Dividing by cd asks "how many lots of cd fit in?" Multiplying by dc answers it. You will use this constantly when finding inverse functions, where a fraction ends up divided by a fraction.

Three errors that look harmless
  • ab + c is not ab + ac. You may split the numerator over a common denominator, never the denominator.
  • a + bb is not a. Cancelling requires a factor of the whole top and the whole bottom, not a term.
  • A minus sign in front of a fraction applies to the whole numerator: −x − 32 = −x + 32.
What to remember
  • Divide by a fraction means multiply by its inverse.
  • You can only cancel factors, never terms.
  • Before cancelling anything, ask what value would make the denominator zero. That value is excluded from the domain.

Powers and roots

Eight rules. Week 2 puts them on a slide and expects them known; the exponential and logarithm work is impossible without them.

xm · xn = xm+n    xmxn = xm−n    (xm)n = xm·n
(xy)m = xmym    (xy)m = xmym
x1 = x    x0 = 1    x−m = 1xm
Squaring and un-squaring are not quite inverse

x2 = |x|, not x. Check it: (−4)2 = 16 = 4, not −4. So when you undo a square with a root you must know the sign of what you are recovering. In this course that sign is normally settled by the context, because a quantity, a price or a distance cannot be negative. Say so in writing when you use it.

Where the rules come from

All of them follow from counting. x3 · x2 is (x·x·x)(x·x), five copies of x, so the exponents add. x0 = 1 because x3x3 is both 1 and x0. A negative exponent is a reciprocal for the same reason. You do not have to memorise eight facts; you have to be able to rebuild them.

One condition runs through all of them. Every rule that divides, and every negative exponent, requires x ≠ 0. In particular 00 and 0−2 are not defined, for exactly the reason no denominator may be zero. The justification above shows it: x3x3 is only equal to 1 when x is not 0.

Roots are fractional powers

x = x12    3x = x13    nxm = xmn

Writing a root as a power is not a cosmetic choice. It is how you differentiate q in week 5, and it is how you handle the week 2 homework function f(m) = 40m − m24 composed with a square root. Whenever a root blocks you, convert it.

Simplify x · x3 ÷ x3

x12 + 3 − 32multiply means add exponents, divide means subtract

The error that costs the most marks in the whole course

(a + b)2 is not a2 + b2. It is a2 + 2ab + b2. In the same family: a + b ≠ a + b, and ln(a + b) ≠ ln a + ln b. Powers, roots and logarithms all distribute over multiplication, never over addition.

What to remember
  • Multiplying adds exponents, dividing subtracts them, a power of a power multiplies them.
  • A root is a fractional power. Convert it whenever it is in the way.
  • Nothing useful distributes over a sum inside a bracket.

Expanding and factoring

Two directions of the same skill. Expanding is mechanical; factoring is the one that needs practice.

Expanding

a(b + c) = ab + ac
(a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd
(a + b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2    (a − b)2 = a2 − 2ab + b2
(a + b)(a − b) = a2 − b2

The last one is worth knowing by sight. When a quadratic has no middle term and a minus between the two squares, it factors instantly: x2 − 9 becomes (x − 3)(x + 3). The minus is essential. x2 + 9 does not factor over the real numbers at all.

Factoring

In this order, every time
  1. Common factor first. 3x2 + 3x − 18 = 3(x2 + x − 6). The course does exactly this on its week 1 slide, and it makes everything after it easier.
  2. Look for a difference of squares. Two terms, both squares, a minus between them.
  3. For x2 + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. For x2 + x − 6: they are +3 and −2, so it factors as (x + 3)(x − 2).
  4. If none of that works, use the quadratic formula from the next section. Factoring is a shortcut, not an obligation.
Factor 3x2 + 3x − 18 completely
3x2 + 3x − 18the expression
3(x2 + x − 6)take out the common factor 3
two numbers: product −6, sum +1+3 and −2 work
3(x + 3)(x − 2)so the roots are −3 and 2
What to remember
  • Always take the common factor out first.
  • a2 − b2 = (a + b)(a − b), on sight.
  • Factoring a quadratic means finding two numbers with a given product and a given sum.
  • A factored expression hands you its roots for free: each bracket set to zero gives one.

Linear equations

Week 1, slide 4. One unknown, power one, one solution.

The general case

For a, b ∈ ℝ with a ≠ 0, the equation ax + b = 0 has exactly one solution, x = −ba. The condition a ≠ 0 is not decoration: if a = 0 the equation is either impossible or always true, and there is nothing to divide by.

Method
  1. Remove brackets and fractions, so every term is plain.
  2. Move every term containing x to one side and every pure number to the other. Moving across the equals sign flips the sign.
  3. Collect: you now have (something) · x = (number).
  4. Divide both sides by the coefficient of x.
  5. Substitute the answer back into the original equation and check.
Solve 3x + 1 = 5x + 7  · the course's own example
3x + 1 = 5x + 7the equation
3x − 5x = 7 − 1x terms to the left, numbers to the right, signs flip
−2x = 6collect both sides
x = −3divide by −2
3(−3) + 1 = −8  and  5(−3) + 7 = −8the check: both sides agree
What to remember
  • Whatever you do, do it to both sides.
  • Crossing the equals sign changes the sign of a term.
  • Dividing by the coefficient is the last step, not the first.
  • Checking costs ten seconds and catches almost every arithmetic slip.

  • Sign of c. In 3x2 + 3x − 18, c = −18, so −4ac = +216. Dropping that minus is the most common error in the topic.

  • Try factoring first; it is faster when it works, and the formula always works.

Equations with the unknown in a denominator

The one kind of equation where you must state what x cannot be, before you solve.

Method
  1. State the restriction first. Any value making a denominator zero is excluded, whatever happens later.
  2. Multiply both sides by the denominators, or cross-multiply if the equation is one fraction equals one fraction.
  3. Solve the resulting polynomial equation.
  4. Discard any solution that is on the excluded list. It is called an extraneous solution, and it is created by step 2, not present in the original.
Solve x + 4x = 15  · week 1 quiz, question 1
x ≠ 0the restriction, stated before anything else
5(x + 4) = xcross-multiply
5x + 20 = xexpand the left
4x = −20collect
x = −5allowed, since −5 ≠ 0
Why the restriction is not a formality

Solve xx − 2 = 2x − 2 and you get x = 2, which is precisely the value the equation forbids. The correct answer is that there is no solution. An exam question built this way is testing one thing only: whether you wrote the restriction down.

What to remember
  • Write the excluded values before you start solving.
  • Cross-multiplication is legal only because you have excluded the zero denominators.
  • Check every solution against the exclusions at the end.

Systems of two equations

Week 1, slides 6 and 7. Two methods, and the course shows both on the same system.

A system asks for the pair (x, y) that satisfies two equations at once. Geometrically it is the point where two lines cross, which is why week 3 returns to it for break-even analysis and market equilibrium.

Method 1 · Elimination

Multiply one or both equations by a constant so that one unknown has opposite coefficients, then add the equations to make it vanish. Best when the coefficients are already close.

Method 2 · Substitution

Isolate one unknown in one equation, then put that expression into the other. Best when one equation already has a lone x or y.

Elimination · 2x + y = 18 and −x + 5y = 2
2x + y = 18keep the first equation as it is
−2x + 10y = 4multiply the second by 2, so the x terms will cancel
11y = 22add the two equations; the x terms vanish
y = 2divide by 11
x = 8put y = 2 back into 2x + y = 18
Substitution · the same system
x = 5y − 2isolate x in the second equation
2(5y − 2) + y = 18substitute into the first
10y − 4 + y = 18expand

A typo on the slide. In the printed elimination example the third line reads 2x + y = 4. It should still be 2x + y = 18; only the second equation was multiplied. The final answer on the slide, y = 2 and x = 8, is correct.

Always check both equations

2(8) + 2 = 18 ✓ and −8 + 5(2) = 2 ✓. A solution that satisfies only one equation is not a solution.

Not every system has one solution

A system of two linear equations is two lines, so there are three possibilities.

  • They cross once: one solution. This is the normal case.
  • They are parallel and distinct: no solution. You notice because both unknowns vanish and you are left with something false, such as 0 = 7.
  • They are the same line: infinitely many solutions. You notice because both unknowns vanish and you are left with something always true, such as 0 = 0.

When the unknowns disappear, read what is left before concluding you made a mistake. Week 3 uses this for market equilibrium, where "no solution" means the two curves never meet.

What to remember
  • Elimination when coefficients line up; substitution when one unknown is already alone.
  • Both methods must give the same pair.
  • Substitute back into both original equations to check.

Inequalities and absolute value

One extra rule compared with equations, and it is the rule everyone forgets.

The sign flip

An inequality behaves exactly like an equation, with one exception: multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the direction. From −2x > 6 you get x < −3, not x > −3.

There is a second exception, and it is the reason the cross-multiplication of the previous section does not carry over. You may only multiply an inequality by something whose sign you know. In x + 4x > 15 you cannot multiply by x, because x may be positive or negative and you do not know which way the sign would turn. Move everything to one side instead and study the sign of the fraction that results. For the same reason you may not square both sides of an inequality.

Absolute value

The absolute value |x| is the distance from zero, so it is never negative. The course defines it in two pieces, which is the definition to use when a variable is inside.

|x| = x  if x ≥ 0     |x| = −x  if x < 0

To evaluate |x − 2| at a value, compute the inside first, then strip the sign. So |10 − 2| = 8, |3 − 2| = 1 and |−3 − 2| = |−5| = 5.

Solving with an absolute value

Because |A| is a distance from zero, each of the three forms splits into a statement with no bars in it.

  1. |A| = k with k ≥ 0 means A = k or A = −k. So |x − 2| = 5 gives x = 7 or x = −3.
  2. |A| < k means −k < A < k, one single interval. So |x − 2| < 3 gives −3 < x − 2 < 3, that is 1 < x < 5: everything within distance 3 of 2.
  3. |A| > k means A > k or A < −k, two separate intervals. So |x − 2| > 3 gives x > 5 or x < −1.

And |A| = k with k < 0 has no solution at all, because a distance is never negative.

What to remember
  • Multiply or divide by a negative, reverse the inequality.
  • Adding or subtracting never reverses it.
  • |x| is a distance: compute the inside, then drop the sign.
  • |A| = k splits into two cases, |A| < k gives one interval, |A| > k gives two.
  • Never multiply an inequality by something whose sign you do not know.

The plane and straight lines

Every graph in the course lives here, and the straight line is the model week 3 builds on.

A point is written (x, y): first how far along the horizontal axis, then how far up the vertical one. The horizontal axis carries the independent variable, the thing you choose, usually a quantity or a time. The vertical axis carries the dependent variable, the thing that results, usually a cost, a revenue or a price.

The straight line

y = mx + b, where m is the slope and b is the value of y when x = 0.

Some sources, including the course's own table of functions, write the same line as f(x) = ax + b. The letter changes, the meaning does not: the coefficient of x is the slope either way.

slope m = change in ychange in x = y2 − y1x2 − x1

The slope is a rate: how much y changes when x goes up by one. In business that is the whole point. If C(q) = 6q + 80000, the slope 6 is the cost of one more unit and the intercept 80 000 is the cost of producing nothing. Week 4 will call that slope a marginal cost and compute it for curves instead of lines, but the meaning does not change.

Positive slope

The line rises to the right. More x, more y. A supply curve.

Negative slope

The line falls to the right. More x, less y. A demand curve.

Zero slope

A horizontal line, y = b. A fixed cost, unchanged by volume.

Undefined slope

A vertical line, x = a. Not a function: one input would have many outputs.

Intercepts

Two substitutions, nothing more

The y-intercept is where the graph crosses the vertical axis: set x = 0 and compute y. The x-intercept is where it crosses the horizontal axis: set y = 0 and solve for x. Week 2 asks for these repeatedly, and that is the entire technique.

Intercepts of y = 2x + 3  · the week 2 example
x = 0 → y = 2(0) + 3 = 3the y-intercept is the point (0, 3)
y = 0 → 0 = 2x + 3 → x = −32the x-intercept is the point (−32, 0)
What to remember
  • y = mx + b: m is the rate of change, b is the starting value.
  • Slope is rise over run, and it is the ancestor of every derivative in this course.
  • y-intercept: set x = 0. x-intercept: set y = 0.

Percentages, growth and money

The arithmetic behind compound interest in week 1 and the whole financial-mathematics block in spring.

A percentage is a multiplier

Increasing by r percent means multiplying by (1 + r), with r written as a decimal. Decreasing by r percent means multiplying by (1 − r). Doing it n times means raising that multiplier to the power n.

Read backwards, the same rule finds a rate from two figures: percentage change = new − oldold, then multiply by 100 to say it as a percent. Sales moving from 200 to 250 is 250 − 200200 = 0.25, a 25% rise. The denominator is always the starting figure, which is why a rise of 25% is undone by a fall of 20%, not of 25%.

Two traps
  • A 20% rise then a 20% fall does not return you to the start. 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, a 4% loss overall. Percentages compose by multiplying, not by adding.
  • Percentage of and percentage point are different. Moving from 4% to 6% is a rise of two percentage points, and a rise of 50%.
What to remember
  • Convert the percentage to a decimal, then to a multiplier (1 ± r).
  • Repeated change is a power, not a multiplication by n.
  • Growth and decline differ only in the sign inside the bracket.

The modelling cycle

Week 1's real subject. Everything else in the year is a technique inside this loop.

A quantitative method is not a formula. It is a way of turning a question somebody actually asked into something that can be computed, and then turning the computation back into an answer they can act on. The course states the workflow as a chain, and it is worth learning as a chain, because most marks are lost at the joins rather than in the middle.

1 · Question

A vague business question. "What price should we charge?"

2 · Model

Name the variables, the parameters and the assumptions. Write a relationship: R(p) = p × q(p).

3 · Data

Feed the model real numbers, with units.

4 · Answer

Compute. This is the part school usually calls "the maths", and it is the smallest part.

5 · Decision

Translate back into a sentence somebody can act on, with its limits stated.

Variables, parameters, assumptions

A variable changes within the problem, like quantity or price. A parameter is fixed for this problem but could differ elsewhere, like a fixed cost of 80 000 or a rate of 2%. An assumption is something you decided to believe, like "demand is linear in price". The first two are given to you. The third is the one that makes a model wrong, and the one worth stating out loud.

  • Every answer needs units and a sentence.

Part II · Functions · Week 1

What a function is

Session 1. The single idea the whole fall semester is built on.

A function is a rule that takes an input and returns at most one output. That is the entire definition, and the words "at most one" are the part that does the work.

The course's definition

A function f : X → Y is a rule that assigns, to each element x in X, at most one element of Y. If something is assigned to x, it is written f(x). The set of x for which f(x) is defined is the domain; the set of values f(x) actually takes is the range.

Input

Also called the independent variable, or x. The thing you choose or observe.

Output

Also called the dependent variable, or y. The thing that results.

The course's first example is a coffee roaster. XY Coffee Co can produce up to 100 tons a day, and its production cost depends on how much it makes.

The four representations

One function, four faces

The same relationship can appear as words ("cost rises with quantity, steeply at the end"), a table (the one above), a graph, or a formula. A learning objective of week 1 is to move between all four. Exam questions routinely give you one and ask for another, and each face makes a different property obvious: a table gives exact values, a graph gives shape, a formula gives prediction, words give meaning.

What is not a function

If one input can give two different outputs, it is not a function. On a graph this is the vertical line test: if any vertical line cuts the curve twice, it fails. A vertical line x = 3 is itself not a function, which is why week 2 notes that it has no y-intercept and a slope that does not exist.

What to remember
  • A function gives at most one output per input.
  • Domain is the set of legal inputs; range is the set of outputs actually produced.
  • Words, table, graph, formula: be able to move in all directions.
  • In business the domain is usually restricted by reality, not by algebra.

Domain and range

Finding a domain is a search for what is forbidden, not for what is allowed.

Method: four things can go wrong, and only four
  1. Division by zero. Set every denominator equal to zero and solve; those values are excluded.
  2. An even root of a negative. Whatever is under a square root must be ≥ 0.
  3. A logarithm of zero or a negative. Whatever is inside a log or ln must be > 0.
  4. A root underneath a fraction bar. If a square root sits in a denominator, what is inside must be strictly greater than 0, because the root has to be non-zero as well as real. In 1q + 3 the condition is q > −3, not q ≥ −3.

If none of the three appears, the domain is all of ℝ. Then, separately, ask whether the context restricts it further: a quantity cannot be negative, a factory has a capacity.

The most common domain error

Simplifying before finding the domain. In x2 − 4x − 2 the fraction reduces to x + 2, which looks defined everywhere. It is not: the original is undefined at x = 2, and the domain is ℝ\{2}. Find the domain from the expression you were given, not from the one you produced.

What to remember
  • Denominators cannot be zero, even roots cannot take negatives, logarithms cannot take zero or negatives.
  • Write the domain as ℝ\{…} or as an interval, and practise both. ℝ\{2} is the same set as ]−∞ ; 2[ ∪ ]2 ; +∞[, and ℝ\{2, −1} is ]−∞ ; −1[ ∪ ]−1 ; 2[ ∪ ]2 ; +∞[. Lecturers use both notations interchangeably.
  • Always take the domain from the original expression.
  • Then ask what the business context forbids on top of that.

Reading a parabola

The parabolic row deserves more than a line, because week 2 asks you to sketch one and week 8 asks you to optimise one. For f(x) = ax2 + bx + c, the sign of a decides the shape. a > 0 gives a U, a trough with a minimum. a < 0 gives an upside-down U, a peak with a maximum.

The turning point is called the vertex, and it sits at x = −b2a. Substitute that back to get its height. The parabola is symmetric about the vertical line through the vertex, so the two x-intercepts, when they exist, are always the same distance from it.

Case-defined functions

One more kind matters in economics: a function with different rules on different parts of its domain. The course calls these case-defined, and they model anything with a threshold, such as a discount that starts at twelve tickets or a tax band.

f(x) = x      if 0 ≤ x < 3
f(x) = x − 1  if 3 ≤ x < 5
f(x) = 4      if 5 ≤ x < 7
How to evaluate one

Find which line's condition your input satisfies, then use only that line. To find f(4): 4 satisfies 3 ≤ x < 5, so f(4) = 4 − 1 = 3. Read the inequality signs carefully, because ≤ and < decide which line owns the boundary value.

What to remember
  • Identify the family before doing anything: it predicts the shape.
  • Rational functions are the ones with excluded values.
  • For a case-defined function, pick the branch first, then compute.

Graphs

A graph is a claim about a relationship. Week 1 sets standards for making one.

Plotting a function means marking every point (x, f(x)). The course notes that in Excel the two acceptable choices are a scatter of points joined by a smoothed curve, or a scatter joined by straight segments, and it gives a rule worth keeping for every chart you ever produce.

How to sketch a function by hand, in four steps
  1. Find the domain, so you know how far the curve is allowed to go.
  2. Find the intercepts. Set x = 0, then set y = 0.
  3. Build a small table of values, five or six points spread across the domain, including one on each side of every intercept and of every excluded value.
  4. Identify the family from the function library: line, parabola, cubic, root, exponential or rational. That tells you the shape to join the points with.

Worked on y = 4x2 − 16: the domain is all of ℝ; the intercepts are (0, −16) and (±2, 0); a = 4 > 0 so it is a U with its lowest point at (0, −16); and at x = ±3 the value is 20 on both sides. Six marks on the page and the curve draws itself.

The standard

A graph must be understandable without reading the text around it. That requires an explicit title, labels on both axes with units, deliberate minimum and maximum on each axis, and a chart type suited to the data.

Where axes mislead

Choosing a vertical axis that starts at 90 rather than 0 turns a 2% rise into a visual doubling. Week 12 of the course returns to this under "how charts mislead", and it is examinable there. For now, decide your axis limits on purpose and be able to say why.

What to remember
  • Title, axis labels, units, sensible limits. Every time.
  • The graph must stand alone.
  • The vertical line test separates functions from other curves.

Exponential functions and the number e

The model of growth: compounding, populations, and everything in the spring financial block.

Definition

f(x) = bx, where b > 0 and b ≠ 1, is the exponential function with base b. It maps ℝ into the strictly positive reals: an exponential is never zero and never negative, whatever x is.

The variable is in the exponent. That is the whole difference from a power function, where the variable is in the base. x2 and 2x behave nothing alike: at x = 10 the first is 100 and the second is 1024, and the gap grows without limit.

Compound interest

S = P · (1 + r)n   where P is the principal, r the annual rate, n the number of years
10 000 CHF at 2%, three years  · the week 1 slide

The number e

Take (1 + 1n)n and let n grow. At n = 1 it is 2; at n = 12, about 2.613; at n = 365, about 2.7146. It creeps towards a limit that never stops mattering:

e ≈ 2.718281828459…

What it means financially. Put 1 CHF to work for one year at a rate of 100%, and let n be the number of times the interest is added during that year. Compounding once gives 2, monthly (n = 12) gives about 2.613, daily (n = 365) gives about 2.7146, and compounding continuously, adding interest at every instant, gives exactly e. So e is not an arbitrary constant. It is what 100% growth becomes when it is applied continuously rather than in steps. The natural exponential function is exp(x) = ex, and it is the one whose derivative is itself, which is why week 5 treats it as the easiest function to differentiate.

What to remember
  • In bx the variable is the exponent; the output is always strictly positive.
  • S = P(1 + r)n is compound growth.
  • e ≈ 2.71828 is the limit of (1 + 1n)n, and it is continuous compounding.
  • exp(x) = ex is "the" exponential function.

The question "what power do I need?", and the tool for getting a variable out of an exponent.

Definition

Part III · Operations on functions · Week 2

Combining functions

Session 2. Four operations, each defined pointwise, which is the only subtlety.

XY Coffee Co splits its production cost into three pieces: fixed costs f(q) = 10 000, materials g(q) = 200q, and salaries h(q) = 5q2. The total cost is the sum of the three functions, written C = f + g + h, meaning that at every quantity, C(q) = f(q) + g(q) + h(q).

That last sentence is the definition of adding functions: you add their outputs, one input at a time. Nothing more clever is happening.

(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)
(f − g)(x) = f(x) − g(x)
(f · g)(x) = f(x) · g(x)
(fg)(x) = f(x)g(x),  provided g(x) ≠ 0
Where the combined function is defined

The sum, the difference and the product of f and g are defined at every x that is in the domain of f and in the domain of g. That common part is called the intersection of the two domains. The quotient carries one further condition, g(x) ≠ 0, so dividing two functions can shrink the domain even when neither original had a problem.

Example: f(x) = x2 + 4 is defined on all of ℝ and h(x) = x + 1 only for x ≥ −1, so f + h is defined only for x ≥ −1.

f(x) = 3x − 1 and g(x) = x2 + 3x  · the week 2 example

(f − g)(x) = −x2 − 1(3x − 1) − (x2 + 3x); the bracket flips both signs, and the 3x cancels
(f · g)(x) = 3x3 + 8x2 − 3x(3x − 1)(x2 + 3x), expanded
(fg)(x) = 3x − 1x2 + 3xexcluded where x2 + 3x = 0, that is x = 0 and x = −3

The subtraction bracket

(f − g)(x) means f(x) − [g(x)], with the whole of g inside the bracket. Forgetting to distribute the minus across every term of g is the most frequent error in this section and in the week 2 quiz.

What to remember
  • Combine outputs at each input; the operations are defined pointwise.
  • Subtraction distributes the minus over the whole second function.

Composition of functions

Feeding one machine's output into another. The operation that makes chains of business logic computable.

XY Coffee Co knows two things. The number of customers depends on advertising: N(x) = 100x, where x is the advertising spend in thousands. And revenue depends on the number of customers: R(n) = 70n + n220. The question Jane actually wants answered is different from both: what does revenue do when we change the advertising budget?

You get there by putting the first function inside the second.

Definition

For f : X → Y and g : Y → Z, the composition g ∘ f : X → Z is defined by (g ∘ f)(x) = g(f(x)). Its domain is the set of x in the domain of f whose output f(x) lies in the domain of g.

In words: x must be legal for the inner function, and what the inner function produces must be legal for the outer one. Take f(x) = x − 4 and g(u) = u. Then (g ∘ f)(x) = x − 4. Every x is legal for f, but f(x) only lands in the domain of g when x − 4 ≥ 0, so the composite is defined only for x ≥ 4. Composing can shrink a domain even when the inner function had no restriction of its own.

Read it from the inside out

g ∘ f is read "g after f". The function written on the right acts first. This is the opposite of reading order, and it is where most composition errors begin.

Two properties

The identity function Id(x) = x changes nothing: f ∘ Id = Id ∘ f = f. And composition is associative: (f ∘ g) ∘ h = f ∘ (g ∘ h). It is not commutative, as the example above shows.

Inverse functions

Running the machine backwards. In business: turning a demand curve into a price curve.

XY Coffee Co's daily demand as a function of retail price is Q(p) = 5 000 000p + 1 − 500 000 for 0 ≤ p ≤ 9, and zero above 9. That answers "how much will we sell at this price?". The inverse answers the question management actually asks: "what price do we need to sell this much?"

Definition

The inverse f−1 satisfies f(f−1(x)) = f−1(f(x)) = x, that is f ∘ f−1 = f−1 ∘ f = Id. Graphically, f−1 is the mirror image of f across the line y = x.

A notation warning

f−1 means the inverse function. It does not mean 1f. The same superscript means two different things depending on whether it sits on a function or on a number, and the course relies on you keeping them apart.

When does an inverse exist?

Only when f never sends two different inputs to the same output. Such a function is called one-to-one (injective). Test it on the graph: if any horizontal line cuts the curve twice, f is not one-to-one and has no inverse on that domain.

f(x) = x2 fails, because f(−3) and f(3) are both 9, and running it backwards from 9 cannot decide between −3 and +3. Restricting the domain repairs it: on x ≥ 0 the function f(x) = x2 is one-to-one and its inverse is x. Every function you are asked to invert in this course is either always increasing or always decreasing on its stated domain, and that is exactly what makes it one-to-one.

Method: four steps, once the function is one-to-one
  1. Write y = f(x).
  2. Solve for x in terms of y. This is the real work, and it is ordinary equation solving from Part 0.
  3. The expression you obtain is f−1(y). Rename the variable if the question wants it in x.
  4. Check by composing: f(f−1(x)) must collapse to x.

The linear case, in general

If f(x) = ax + b multiplies by a then adds b, its inverse must subtract b then divide by a, in that order: f−1(x) = x − ba. Undoing a chain of operations means reversing both the operations and their order, exactly as taking off shoes and socks reverses putting them on.

What to remember
  • An inverse undoes: f−1(f(x)) = x.
  • f−1 is not 1f.
  • Method: write y = f(x), solve for x, rename, check by composing.
  • Domain and range trade places.
  • Reverse the operations and their order.

The false rules

ln(a + b) ≠ ln a + ln b. ln(a) · ln(b) ≠ ln(ab). ln(a)ln(b) ≠ ln(a − b). Logarithms convert multiplication into addition, and there is no rule at all for the logarithm of a sum. When you see a sum inside a logarithm, the only legal move is to factor it first.

And a fourth, which is subtler: ln(x2) is not always 2 ln x. The power rule logb(mr) = r · logb m holds only when m > 0. For x < 0 the quantity x2 is positive but ln x does not exist. Correctly, ln(x2) = 2 ln|x|. Before pulling an exponent out in front, check that what is inside is strictly positive.

What to remember
  • Product becomes sum, quotient becomes difference, power becomes a multiplier in front.
  • exp and ln undo each other; that is how you free a variable from an exponent.
  • There is no rule for the logarithm of a sum.
  • Convert every root into a fractional power before applying any rule.

1.1 · zero profit

Revenue 100q, variable cost 2 per unit, fixed cost 1200

Find the values of q for which total revenue equals total cost.

Method
  1. Write both sides: revenue 100q, cost 2q + 1200. Set them equal.
  2. Divide through by 2 to make the numbers smaller before squaring. This is optional but halves the arithmetic.
  3. Square both sides to remove the root. Squaring can introduce false solutions, so check at the end.
  4. You now have a quadratic. Move everything to one side and factor. With b = −1300 and c = +360 000 the two numbers are both negative: −400 and −900.

Checkpoint. q = 400 or q = 900. Two break-even points. Interpret them: revenue exceeds cost only between 400 and 900 units. Below 400 the fixed cost of 1 200 has not been recovered. Above 900 the linear cost has overtaken the flattening revenue curve, because 100q grows more and more slowly while 2q does not. A curve against a line can cross twice, once, or not at all, so always solve rather than assume.

1.2 · two pay scales

  2. "Better" means greater, so write an inequality, not an equation.
  3. Expand, collect, and divide. Watch whether you divide by a positive or a negative number.

  2. For part b, invert the composite: subtract the 1, take the reciprocal, then subtract the 3.

Checkpoints. (a) p(w) = 1w + 3 + 1. (b) w(p) = 1p − 1 − 3. If part a gives you something with a w still in it, you have read the second relation as w + 1 rather than w + 1; the clean cancellation is the sign that the second reading is intended.

2.11 · a demand function

P(q) = 2q + 1 + 3 for q > 0

Method
  1. Peel the layers in reverse: subtract 3, divide by 2, square, subtract 1.
  2. For part b, substitute Q(p) into P and simplify until only p remains. The square root and the square must annihilate each other.

Checkpoints. (a) Q(p) = (p − 3)24 − 1. (b) substituting gives 2(p − 3)24 + 3 = 2 · |p − 3|2 + 3, and since P(q) ≥ 3 for every q > 0 we know p ≥ 3, so |p − 3| = p − 3 and the whole thing collapses to p, the identity. Writing the absolute value is not pedantry: A2 = |A| in general, and the exam expects the justification of the sign.

2.12 · MIC-ADO

Meals = 1000restaurants + 1 + 100, Revenue = Meals2 − 500

Express the number of nearby restaurants as a function of revenue, then find it when revenue is 89 500.

Method
  1. This is a composition you must run backwards, through two functions. Undo the outer one first: from revenue, recover meals by adding 500 and taking the square root.
  2. Then undo the inner one: subtract 100, and invert the fraction to free the number of restaurants.
  3. For part b, substitute and keep an eye out for the clean numbers, which confirm the algebra.

Checkpoints. (a) R = 1000Revenue + 500 − 100 − 1. (b) 90 000 = 300, then 1000200 = 5, so 4 restaurants. Verify forwards: 4 restaurants give 300 meals, and 3002 − 500 = 89 500. That round trip is the check to run on every inverse question.

Where marks are lost

Each one paired with the habit that prevents it.

1 · Distributing over a sum

(a + b)2 ≠ a2 + b2, and the same for roots and logarithms.

Habit: whenever a sum sits inside a power, root or logarithm, stop and expand or factor properly.

2 · The sign of c in the discriminant

With c = −18, −4ac is +216.

Habit: write a =, b =, c = in a column with signs before touching the formula.

3 · Dividing by x

It destroys the solution x = 0.

Habit: factor instead of dividing, always.

4 · Forgetting the domain

Simplifying hides an excluded value that was there in the original.

Habit: write the exclusions from the given expression, before simplifying anything.

5 · Composition in the wrong order

g ∘ f is not f ∘ g.

Habit: say "g after f" aloud, and substitute the right-hand function first.

6 · Substituting without brackets

(2p + 1)2, never 2p2 + 1.

Habit: write the bracket before you write anything inside it.

7 · Reading f−1 as 1f

They are different objects.

Habit: check your inverse by composing; if it does not collapse to x, it is wrong.

8 · The minus in a subtraction of functions

It applies to every term of the second function.

Habit: write f(x) − [g(x)] with the bracket, then distribute.

9 · log instead of ln

Two different keys, two different answers, no warning.

Habit: circle which one the question uses before reaching for the calculator.

10 · Percentages added rather than compounded

Up 20% then down 20% is a 4% loss.

Habit: convert every percentage to a multiplier and multiply them.

11 · Calculator precedence

10 ÷ 2 + 3 is 8, not 2.

Habit: bracket every numerator and every denominator, even when it looks unnecessary.

12 · Answering with a bare number

A quantity without units or a sentence is an incomplete answer in an open-question exam.

Habit: finish every applied question with one sentence naming the quantity and its unit.

When you are stuck in the exam

Write down what you are looking for and what you have been given, in words, with units. Most blocked questions in this course are blocked at the modelling step, not the algebra step, and naming the variable usually unblocks them. If it does not, move on and come back: in a 60-minute MCQ, five minutes on one question costs you two others.

The two things that carry the whole year

First, the idea of a function: a rule from input to output, with a domain. Derivatives measure how it changes, integrals accumulate it, a regression estimates it from data, and a matrix holds many of them at once. Second, the modelling cycle: question, model, data, answer, decision. Every exam question is a small trip around that loop, and the marks are mostly at the two ends, where words become mathematics and mathematics becomes advice.
