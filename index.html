Numbers, notation and the order of operations

Not started

The symbols the lecturer writes without explaining, and the order every calculation follows.

Khan AcademyWatch: Irrational numbersWatch: Classifying numbers: rational & irrationalRead: Order of operations reviewPractise: Order of operationsPractise: Order of operations with fractions and exponents
The four sets
ℕ whole counting numbers, ℤ those and their negatives, ℚ anything writable as a fraction of integers, ℝ every point on the line. Each contains the one before it: ℕ ⊂ ℤ ⊂ ℚ ⊂ ℝ
Reading the symbols
x ∈ ℝ says x is a real number. ⊂ is included in, ≠ is not equal, ≤ at most, ≥ at least, ∀ for every, ∃ there exists.
No solution in ℝ
Means no solution among the real numbers. This course stays in ℝ all year, so that is the whole answer.
  1. Order of operations. 1. Brackets  ›  2. Powers and roots  ›  3. × and ÷  ›  4. + and −
  2. Same rank, left to right. 12 ÷ 3 × 2 = 8, not 2.
  3. A minus in front is not part of the base. −32 = −9 because the square binds first. (−3)2 = 9 because the bracket binds first.
  4. Round once, at the end. Carry at least four significant figures through the working. Rounding at each step moves the final answer.
Evaluate 10 − 2 × 32
  1. 32 = 9
    Powers before multiplication.
  2. 2 × 9 = 18
    Then the multiplication.
  3. 10 − 18 = −8
    Subtraction last.
  • Traps
  • −32 is −9. Only a bracket makes it 9.
  • (10 − 2) × 32 = 72. The bracket changes the answer entirely.
  • Rounding an intermediate figure to two decimals and then compounding it is the commonest arithmetic slip in this course.

Fractions and signs

Not started

One bar, four operations, and the cancellation rule that decides most marks.

Khan AcademyWatch: Dividing fractions by fractionsWatch: Understanding multiplying and dividing negative fractionsPractise: Add and subtract fractionsPractise: Dividing fractions
  1. Multiply. Straight across, no preparation: ab × cd = acbd
  2. Divide. Turn the second one over and multiply: ab ÷ cd = ab × dc = adbc
  3. Add or subtract. Only these two need a common denominator, because you can only add halves to halves.
  4. Simplify. Cancel factors, never terms. A factor multiplies the whole numerator or the whole denominator.
  5. The minus sign. −ab = a−b = −ab. Put it in front and keep it there.
Simplify x2 − 9x + 3
  1. (x − 3)(x + 3)x + 3
    Factor first. Now x + 3 is a factor, so it may go.
  2. x − 3
    Valid for x ≠ −3, because the original was undefined there.
  • Traps
  • x + 3x is not 3. The x in the numerator is a term, not a factor, so it does not cancel.
  • Test any cancellation with numbers. At x = 1: 1 + 31 = 4, not 3.
  • −(x − 3) = −x + 3. The minus reaches both terms.

Powers and roots

Not started

Five rules, one definition of a root, and where fractional exponents appear in the business.

Khan AcademyWatch: Exponent properties reviewWatch: Simplifying square rootsPractise: Properties of exponents challenge (integer exponents)Practise: Simplify square roots
The rules
am · an = am+n    aman = am−n    (am)n = amn(ab)n = anbn    a0 = 1    a−n = 1an
Roots are powers
a = a1/2    na = a1/n    nam = am/nSo every root obeys the five rules above. There is nothing extra to learn.
Where it is defined
An even root needs a non-negative inside. 00 and division by zero are undefined.
Simplify (2x3)2 · x4x2
  1. 4x6 · x4x2
    (2x3)2 = 22x6. The power reaches the 2 as well.
  2. 4x74x2
    Add the exponents on top.
  3. x5
    Subtract them underneath.
  • Traps
  • (2x)3 = 8x3, not 2x3. The exponent reaches the coefficient.
  • a + b is not a + b. Try a = 9, b = 16: 5 against 7.
  • x2 is |x|, not x. Squaring then un-squaring loses the sign.

Expanding and factoring

Not started

The same three identities, read left to right and then right to left.

Khan AcademyWatch: Multiplying binomialsWatch: Multiplying binomials introRead: Warmup: Multiplying binomialsPractise: Multiply binomialsPractise: Multiply difference of squares
The three identities
(a + b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2(a − b)2 = a2 − 2ab + b2(a + b)(a − b) = a2 − b2Expanding reads them left to right. Factoring reads them right to left.
  1. Expanding two brackets. Every term in the first meets every term in the second. Four products for two binomials.
  2. Factoring, in this order. Common factor first, then a recognised identity, then the sum-and-product search.
  3. Sum and product. For x2 + bx + c, find two numbers multiplying to c and adding to b.
  4. Check by expanding. Factoring is only correct if multiplying back returns the original.
Factor x2 − 5x + 6
  1. two numbers with product 6, sum −5
    Try the pairs of 6: 1 · 6 and 2 · 3.
  2. −2 and −3
    (−2)(−3) = 6 and (−2) + (−3) = −5.
  3. (x − 2)(x − 3)
    Expand back to check: it returns the original.
  • Traps
  • (a + b)2 is not a2 + b2. The 2ab is the whole point.
  • Take the common factor out first. 2x2 − 8 is 2(x2 − 4) = 2(x − 2)(x + 2).
  • a2 + b2 does not factor over the reals. Only the difference does.

Linear equations

Not started

One unknown, first power only. The procedure never changes.

Khan AcademyWatch: Linear equations with variables on both sidesWatch: Linear equations with parenthesesRead: Multi-step equations reviewPractise: Equations with variables on both sidesPractise: Equations with parentheses
  1. Clear brackets. Distribute over everything inside.
  2. Clear fractions. Multiply every term by the common denominator, including the terms that have none.
  3. Collect. All x terms on one side, all numbers on the other. Whatever you do, do to both sides.
  4. Divide. By the coefficient of x.
  5. Check. Put the answer back into the original equation, not into your own line 3.
Solve 3(x − 2) = 2x + 7
  1. 3x − 6 = 2x + 7
    Brackets cleared.
  2. 3x − 2x = 7 + 6
    x terms left, numbers right.
  3. x = 13
    Check: 3(13 − 2) = 33 and 2(13) + 7 = 33.
When x disappears
If you reach 0 = 0 every number is a solution. If you reach something false such as 0 = 5 there is no solution. Neither is a mistake.
Making another letter the subject
Same five steps, treating every other letter as a number. P = R − C gives C = R − P.
  • Traps
  • Multiplying by a denominator means multiplying every term. x2 + 1 = 5 becomes x + 2 = 10.
  • −(x − 4) = −x + 4. Losing that second sign is the commonest slip here.

Quadratic equations

Not started

Anything with an x2 in it. Everything to one side first, always.

Khan AcademyWatch: Solving quadratics by factoringWatch: Solving quadratics by factoring: leading coefficient ≠ 1Read: Solving quadratics by factoring reviewPractise: Quadratics by factoringPractise: Quadratic formula
The form and the formula
ax2 + bx + c = 0,   a ≠ 0x = −b ± b2 − 4ac2a
The discriminant
Δ = b2 − 4ac counts the real solutions before you find them: Δ > 0 two, Δ = 0 one, Δ < 0 none in ℝ.
  1. Move everything to one side. The formula and the discriminant are only valid against zero.
  2. Read off a, b and c. With their signs. A missing term means that coefficient is 0.
  3. Try factoring first. If two numbers multiply to c and add to b, you are done in one line.
  4. Otherwise use the formula. Compute Δ first, then decide whether to continue.
  5. In a business question, choose the root. A negative quantity or a negative price is not an answer.
Solve x2 + 2x − 8 = 0
  1. Δ = 22 − 4(1)(−8) = 4 + 32 = 36
    Positive, so two real roots.
  2. x = −2 ± 62
    36 = 6.
  3. x = 2 or x = −4
    Check x = 2: 4 + 4 − 8 = 0.
  • Traps
  • x2 + 2x = 8 is not ready. Move the 8 first, or c is wrong.
  • In −4ac with c negative, the two minus signs make a plus. This is where most discriminants go wrong.
  • Δ < 0 means stop and write that there is no solution in ℝ. It is a complete answer.

Equations with the unknown in a denominator

Not started

The same algebra, plus one compulsory check at the end.

Khan AcademyWatch: Equations with rational expressionsWatch: Equations with rational expressions (example 2)Read: Extraneous solutions of radical equationsPractise: Rational equations introPractise: Extraneous solutions of equations
  1. State what is forbidden. Before anything else, write down the values that make any denominator zero.
  2. Multiply through. By the common denominator, so every fraction disappears.
  3. Solve what is left. It is usually linear or quadratic.
  4. Reject candidates. Any answer matching a forbidden value is discarded. It is not a solution.
Solve 2x − 1 = 3x
  1. x ≠ 1 and x ≠ 0
    The forbidden values, written before solving.
  2. 2x = 3(x − 1)
    Cross-multiply, legitimate because neither side is zero.
  3. 2x = 3x − 3 → x = 3
    Allowed, since 3 is neither 0 nor 1.
  • Traps
  • An answer that makes a denominator zero is rejected, not written down with a note. It is simply not a solution.
  • If every candidate is rejected, the equation has no solution. That is a valid final answer.
  • Cross-multiplication is only for a single fraction equal to a single fraction. With three terms, use the common denominator.

Systems of two equations

Not started

Two facts, two unknowns. Two methods, and the check uses both equations.

Khan AcademyWatch: Solving systems of equations with substitutionWatch: Systems of equations with substitution: -3x-4y=-2 & y=2x-5Practise: Systems of equations with substitutionPractise: Systems of equations with elimination
  1. Substitution. Isolate whichever letter has a coefficient of 1 or −1, then put that expression into the other equation.
  2. Elimination. Multiply one or both equations so that one letter has equal and opposite coefficients, then add.
  3. Solve, then back-substitute. One equation now has one unknown. Find it, then recover the other.
  4. Check in both. A pair that satisfies only one equation is not a solution.
The three outcomes
One solution, the usual case. No solution, when the lines are parallel and you reach something false. Infinitely many, when the two equations are the same line and you reach something always true.
Solve 2x + y = 7 and x − y = 2
  1. 3x = 9
    Add them: the y terms cancel without any multiplying.
  2. x = 3
    Then back-substitute.
  3. y = 1
    Check both: 2(3) + 1 = 7 and 3 − 1 = 2.
  • Traps
  • When you multiply an equation, multiply its right-hand side too.
  • Checking in one equation only will pass an answer that is wrong.

Inequalities and absolute value

Not started

The answer is a set of numbers, not a number.

Khan AcademyWatch: Multi-step inequalitiesWatch: Double inequalitiesRead: Compound inequalities reviewPractise: Multi-step linear inequalitiesPractise: Compound inequalities
  1. Solve as an equation. Add, subtract, multiply and divide exactly as usual.
  2. Two exceptions. Multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses the sign, and so does taking reciprocals of both sides.
  3. Unknown sign means split. If you would divide by a without knowing its sign, do two cases instead.
  4. Write the answer as a set. x > 3, or an interval, or on a number line. All three are accepted.
Absolute value is a distance
|x| is the distance from x to 0, so it is never negative.
The three forms
|x| = k   →   x = k or x = −k|x| < k   →   −k < x < k|x| > k   →   x < −k or x > k
Solve −2x + 1 ≤ 7
  1. −2x ≤ 6
    Subtracting 1 changes nothing about the direction.
  2. x ≥ −3
    Dividing by −2 reverses it.
  • Traps
  • Dividing by a negative and forgetting to turn the sign is the single commonest error in this topic.
  • |x| < −2 has no solution. A distance is never negative.
  • |x| > k gives two separate rays, joined by or, not one interval.

The plane and straight lines

Not started

Slope is a rate of change. Three forms of the same line.

Khan AcademyWatch: Worked examples: slope-intercept introWatch: Horizontal & vertical linesRead: Point-slope form reviewPractise: Slope-intercept equation from graphPractise: Point-slope form
Slope
m = ΔyΔx = y2 − y1x2 − x1It is a rate: how much y changes per one unit of x.
The three forms
y = mx + h    slope-intercepty − y1 = m(x − x1)    point-slopeax + by = c    standard
Parallel and perpendicular
Parallel means m1 = m2. Perpendicular means m1m2 = −1.
  1. From two points. Compute the slope, then use point-slope with either point. Both give the same line.
  2. Horizontal and vertical. y = k is horizontal with slope 0. x = k is vertical and has no slope at all.
  3. Reading a business line. In C = 5q + 2000 the slope 5 is the cost of one more unit and the intercept 2000 is the fixed cost.
  • Traps
  • ΔyΔx, never ΔxΔy. Rise over run.
  • A vertical line has undefined slope, which is not the same as slope zero.
  • Subtract the coordinates in the same order top and bottom, or the sign comes out wrong.

Percentages, growth and money

Not started

One idea: a percentage change is a multiplier. Everything else follows.

Khan AcademyWatch: Percent problemsWatch: Exponential growth & decayRead: Converting percents and fractions reviewPractise: Percent word problemsPractise: Exponential expressions word problems (numerical)
The multiplier
A rise of r per cent multiplies by 1 + r100. A fall multiplies by 1 − r100. A 20% rise is × 1.2; a 20% fall is × 0.8.
Repeated change
final = initial × (1 + r100)nChanges multiply, they never add. Up 20% then down 20% gives 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, a 4% loss.
Percentage change from two figures
new − oldold × 100Always divided by the old figure.
Points against per cent
From 5% to 7% is a rise of 2 percentage points, and a rise of 40 per cent. Both are true and they are different numbers.
  1. Find the rate from a start and an end. r = ((finalinitial)1/n − 1) × 100
  2. Find the number of periods. Solve (1 + r100)n = finalinitial with logarithms. That is week 2.
  3. Simple against compound. Simple interest pays on the original only: A = P(1 + rn). Compound pays on the running total: A = P(1 + r)n.
1000 CHF at 5% a year for 3 years, compound
  1. 1000 × 1.053
    Three multiplications by the same multiplier.
  2. 1157.63 CHF
    Simple interest would give 1000(1 + 0.05 × 3) = 1150.
  • Traps
  • A 20% fall does not undo a 20% rise.
  • Percentage change always divides by the old figure, not the new one and not the difference.
  • Percentage points and per cent are different units. Exam questions test the difference deliberately.

The modelling cycle

Not started

Turning a business question into maths, and turning the answer back into a sentence.

Khan AcademyWatch: Rate conversionWatch: Using units to solve problems: Road tripRead: Working with units: FAQPractise: Formulas and unitsPractise: Using units to solve problems
  1. Name the variables. What varies, in what unit, over what range. Write it down before any algebra.
  2. Separate parameters from variables. A parameter is fixed for this question, such as a price. A variable is what you are solving for.
  3. Write the relationships. Usually from the three below.
  4. Solve. Ordinary algebra from here.
  5. Interpret. Convert the number back into the language of the question, with its unit. A bare number is not an answer.
The three relationships to know
revenue = price × quantitycost = fixed + variable × quantityprofit = revenue − cost
Units
Units carry through the algebra. If the answer comes out in the wrong unit the model is wrong, which makes units the fastest check you have.
A cafe sells coffee at 4.50 CHF, with 1200 CHF of fixed monthly costs and 1.20 CHF of beans a cup. Break even?
  1. R(q) = 4.50q,   C(q) = 1200 + 1.20q
    Two functions of the same variable q, cups a month.
  2. 4.50q = 1200 + 1.20q
    Break-even is where they meet.
  3. q = 363.6, so 364 cups
    Cups are whole, and 363 would still be a loss.
  • Traps
  • Rounding down a break-even quantity gives an answer that still loses money. Round up.
  • A quantity that comes out negative or fractional when it cannot be means the model needs re-reading, not forcing.

What a function is

Not started

A rule that gives each input at most one output.

Khan AcademyWatch: Worked example: Evaluating functions from equationWatch: Worked example: Evaluating functions from graphPractise: Evaluate functionsPractise: Function notation word problems
The definition
A function f from a set to a set assigns to each input at most one output. At most one, not exactly one: some inputs may be forbidden, and those form the gap between the set and the domain.
The notation
f(x) is the output at x. It is not f multiplied by x. f is the rule; f(x) is one number.
Four ways to hold the same function
A formula, a graph, a table, a sentence. Exam questions move between them, and the function is the same object throughout.
  1. Evaluating at a number. Replace every x by that number, brackets included.
  2. Evaluating at an expression. Replace every x by the whole expression, in brackets. This is what composition and the difference quotient need later.
  3. The vertical line test. If any vertical line meets the graph twice, it is not a function, because that input would have two outputs.
  4. Case-defined functions. Read which interval the input falls in first, then use only that line. Check which case owns the boundary.
f(x) = x2 − 3x, evaluate at 4 and at a + 1
  1. f(4) = 42 − 3(4) = 4
    Straight substitution.
  2. f(a + 1) = (a + 1)2 − 3(a + 1)
    The whole expression, in brackets, in both places.
  3. = a2 − a − 2
    Expand: a2 + 2a + 1 − 3a − 3.
  • Traps
  • f(a + 1) is not f(a) + 1.
  • f(x)2 means the output squared; f(x2) means the input squared. Different numbers.

Domain and range

Not started

The domain is every input that is allowed. Hunt for what is forbidden.

Khan AcademyWatch: What is the domain of a function?Watch: What is the range of a function?Practise: Domain and range from graphPractise: Determine the domain of functions
  1. Assume everything is allowed. Start from ℝ and remove.
  2. Denominator zero. Set each denominator to zero and exclude those values.
  3. Even root negative. Set the inside ≥ 0 and solve.
  4. Logarithm not positive. Set the inside > 0 and solve. Strict, because ln 0 does not exist.
  5. Combine. When two conditions apply, the domain is where both hold at once, not either.
  6. Context can restrict further. A number of employees cannot be 7.3 or negative, whatever the algebra allows.
Three ways to write it
x ≥ 4, or [4, ∞[, or a shaded ray on a number line. All three are accepted.
The range
The set of outputs actually reached. Read it off the graph rather than the formula: find the lowest and highest values the curve attains.
Domain of f(x) = x − 2x − 5
  1. x − 2 ≥ 0 → x ≥ 2
    The root.
  2. x − 5 ≠ 0 → x ≠ 5
    The denominator.
  3. x ≥ 2 and x ≠ 5
    Both conditions at once.
  • Traps
  • A root needs ≥ 0 but a logarithm needs > 0. The difference is examined.
  • Two conditions means both, not either. Take the intersection.
  • Simplifying first can hide a restriction. Read the domain off the original formula.

The function library

Not started

Recognise the family from the formula, and you know the shape before you draw it.

Khan AcademyWatch: Worked example: graphing piecewise functionsWatch: Zeros of polynomialsRead: Zeros of polynomials & their graphsPractise: Evaluate piecewise functionsPractise: Piecewise functions graphs
The families you must recognise on sight
FamilyFormShape
Constantf(x) = chorizontal line
Linearf(x) = mx + hstraight line, slope m
Quadraticf(x) = ax2 + bx + cparabola, opens up if a > 0

Power against exponential
x2 is a power: the variable is the base. 2x is exponential: the variable is the exponent. They behave completely differently, and mixing them up is the commonest error in this topic.
Reading a parabola
a decides the opening and the width. The vertex sits at x = −b2a and the curve is symmetric about that vertical line, which gives you a second point free for every point you find.
Case-defined
A function may be built from several pieces, each on its own interval. It is still one function.
  • Traps
  • 2x at x = 10 is 1024; x2 is 100. Exponentials overtake every power eventually.
  • The vertex formula is −b2a, with the sign of b as written in the equation.

Graphs

Not started

Sketching by hand, and reading information back off a curve.

Khan AcademyWatch: Shifting functionsWatch: Reflecting functionsRead: Transformations of functions: FAQPractise: Shift functionsPractise: Reflect functions
  1. Find the intercepts. Set x = 0 for the y-intercept, set y = 0 for the x-intercepts.
  2. Find the special points. The vertex of a parabola, the breaks of a rational function, the starting point of a root.
  3. Mark what is forbidden. Draw the vertical asymptote as a dashed line so the curve cannot cross it by accident.
  4. Plot a few more points. Three or four is usually enough once you know the family.
  5. Label both axes. With the quantity and its unit. An unlabelled graph earns no marks in this course.
Reading back
Where the curve crosses the horizontal axis is where the quantity is zero, which in business is usually break-even. Where two curves cross is where the two quantities are equal.
A truncated axis exaggerates
A vertical axis that starts at 90 rather than 0 makes a 2% change look enormous. Check where the axis starts before believing the shape.
  • Traps
  • A sketch still needs the intercepts and the vertex marked with their values. A shape alone is not an answer.
  • A curve must never cross its own vertical asymptote.

Exponential functions and the number e

Not started

The variable sits in the exponent. That one fact drives everything.

Khan AcademyWatch: Exponential growth & decayWatch: Graphing exponential growth & decayRead: Exponential growth & decay: FAQPractise: Exponential expressions word problems (numerical)Practise: Exponential expressions word problems (algebraic)
The form
f(x) = a · bx,   b > 0, b ≠ 1a is the value at x = 0. b is the multiplier for one step.
The two shapes
b > 1 grows, 0 < b < 1 decays. Every exponential passes through (0, a) and never touches the horizontal axis.
Building b from a percentage
A rise of r per cent per period gives b = 1 + r100. A fall gives b = 1 − r100.
The number e
e ≈ 2.71828. It is the limit of (1 + 1n)n as n grows, which is what compounding more and more often approaches. Continuous growth is written A = Pert.
Subscribers start at 500 and grow 8% a month. How many after 6 months?
  1. N(t) = 500 · 1.08t
    The multiplier is 1.08, not 0.08.
  2. N(6) = 500 · 1.086
    Six months.
  3. 793 subscribers

Four places where Khan writes it differently

Khan Academy is American, and a few conventions differ from the ones your exam expects. None of them is a disagreement about the mathematics.

Intervals. Khan writes an open end as a round bracket, (2, 5]. Your course writes it reversed, ]2 ; 5]. Both mean the same set.

Thousands and decimals. Khan writes 10,000.5. Your course writes 10 000.5, with a space.

The order of operations. Khan says PEMDAS, your course states the rule in words. Same rule.

Function definition. Khan usually says a function gives exactly one output per input. Your course says at most one, which is what lets a domain exclude values. Follow your course on this one.

Where marks are lost

Each one paired with the habit that prevents it.

1 · Distributing over a sum

(a + b)2 ≠ a2 + b2, and the same for roots and logarithms.

Habit: whenever a sum sits inside a power, root or logarithm, stop and expand or factor properly.

2 · The sign of c in the discriminant

With c = −18, −4ac is +216.

Habit: write a =, b =, c = in a column with signs before touching the formula.

3 · Dividing by x

It destroys the solution x = 0.

Habit: factor instead of dividing, always.

4 · Forgetting the domain

Simplifying hides an excluded value that was there in the original.

Habit: write the exclusions from the given expression, before simplifying anything.

5 · Composition in the wrong order

g ∘ f is not f ∘ g.

Habit: say "g after f" aloud, and substitute the right-hand function first.

6 · Substituting without brackets

(2p + 1)2, never 2p2 + 1.

Habit: write the bracket before you write anything inside it.

7 · Reading f−1 as 1f

They are different objects.

Habit: check your inverse by composing; if it does not collapse to x, it is wrong.

8 · The minus in a subtraction of functions

It applies to every term of the second function.

Habit: write f(x) − [g(x)] with the bracket, then distribute.

9 · log instead of ln

Two different keys, two different answers, no warning.

Habit: circle which one the question uses before reaching for the calculator.

10 · Percentages added rather than compounded

Up 20% then down 20% is a 4% loss.

Habit: convert every percentage to a multiplier and multiply them.

11 · Calculator precedence

10 ÷ 2 + 3 is 8, not 2.

Habit: bracket every numerator and every denominator, even when it looks unnecessary.

12 · Answering with a bare number

A quantity without units or a sentence is an incomplete answer in an open-question exam.

Habit: finish every applied question with one sentence naming the quantity and its unit.

When you are stuck in the exam

Write down what you are looking for and what you have been given, in words, with units. Most blocked questions in this course are blocked at the modelling step, not the algebra step, and naming the variable usually unblocks them. If it does not, move on and come back: in a 60-minute MCQ, five minutes on one question costs you two others.
