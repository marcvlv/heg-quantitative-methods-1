Powers and roots

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: The five rules · Exponent properties reviewWatch: Simplifying square rootsPractise: The rules together · Properties of exponents challenge (integer exponents)Practise: Simplify square roots
  1. Same base, multiplied: add the exponents. am · an = am+n
  2. Same base, divided: subtract them. aman = am−n
  3. A power of a power: multiply them. (am)n = amn
  4. A product inside a power: the power reaches every factor. (ab)n = anbn
  5. Zero and negative exponents are definitions, not tricks. a0 = 1(a ≠ 0)a−n = 1an
  6. A root is a fractional exponent, which is why the same five rules cover roots. a = a1/2the n-th root of a = a1/n
Simplify (2x3)44x5
  1. 16x124x5
    The fourth power reaches the 2 as well as the x³, so 2⁴ = 16 and x¹².
  2. 4x7
    Divide the numbers, subtract the exponents.
  • Watch out
  • (a + b)2 is not a2 + b2. A power does not reach across a plus.
  • a−n is not negative. It is a reciprocal, so 2−3 = 18.
  • a2 + b2 does not split into a + b. Test it at a = b = 3.

Working with algebraic expressions

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: Expanding, from the start · Multiplying binomials introWatch: Two brackets · Multiplying binomialsRead: Worked through · Warmup: Multiplying binomialsPractise: Expanding · Multiply binomialsPractise: The difference of two squares · Multiply difference of squares
Term
What the + and − signs separate. In 3x2 − 5x + 2 there are three terms.
Factor
What multiplies the whole of something. Factors cancel; terms never do.
Like terms
Same letter, same power. 3x and 5x are alike; 3x and 3x2 are not.
  1. Distribute across every term in the bracket. a(b + c) = ab + ac
  2. Two brackets: every term of the first against every term of the second. (a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd
  3. Two patterns are worth knowing by sight. (a + b)(a − b) = a2 − b2(a ± b)2 = a2 ± 2ab + b2
  4. A minus in front of a bracket reaches every term inside it. −(x − 4) = −x + 4
  5. Collect like terms last. Nothing else combines.
Expand (2x − 5)(3x + 4)
  1. 6x2 + 8x − 15x − 20
    Each term of the first bracket against each term of the second.
  2. 6x2 − 7x − 20
    Collect the two x terms.
  • Watch out
  • 2(x + 3) is 2x + 6, not 2x + 3. The 2 reaches both terms.
  • (q + 3)2 is q2 + 6q + 9. The middle term is the one people drop.
  • 3x and 3x2 do not combine. Different power, different term.

Factoring

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: What factoring is · Introduction to factoringWatch: Factoring a quadratic · Factoring quadratics introWatch: The difference of two squares · Factoring quadratics with difference of squaresRead: The method, written out · Factoring simple quadratics reviewPractise: Common factor first · GCF factoring introductionPractise: Factoring a quadratic · Factoring quadratics intro
  1. Take out the common factor first, every time. 6x2 + 9x = 3x(2x + 3)
  2. For x² + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. x2 − 13x + 36 = (x − 4)(x − 9)
  3. Difference of two squares. a2 − b2 = (a − b)(a + b)
  4. Perfect square. a2 ± 2ab + b2 = (a ± b)2
  5. When the leading coefficient is not 1, split the middle term and group. Find two numbers multiplying to ac and adding to b.
  6. Check by expanding. It costs ten seconds and catches every sign error.
Factor 3x2 + 11x − 4
  1. ac = 3 × (−4) = −12
    Two numbers multiplying to −12 and adding to 11: 12 and −1.
  2. 3x2 + 12x − x − 4
    Split the middle term using those two numbers.
  3. 3x(x + 4) − 1(x + 4)
    Group in pairs and take each common factor out.
  4. (3x − 1)(x + 4)
    The bracket x + 4 is now itself the common factor.
  • Watch out
  • x2 + 9 does not factor over the real numbers. A sum of two squares never does.
  • Missing the common factor first makes every later step harder and sometimes impossible.
  • 4x2 − 16 is 4(x − 2)(x + 2). Completely means completely.

Fractions

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: Dividing fractions by fractionsPractise: Adding and subtracting · Add and subtract fractionsPractise: Dividing fractions
  1. Multiply straight across. No preparation needed. ab × cd = acbd
  2. Divide by turning the second one over. ab ÷ cd = ab × dc = adbc
  3. Only adding and subtracting need a common denominator. You can add halves to halves, not halves to thirds.
  4. Cancel factors, never terms. A factor multiplies the whole numerator or the whole denominator.
  5. Keep the minus sign in front. −ab = a−b = −ab
Simplify x2 − 25x + 5
  1. (x − 5)(x + 5)x + 5
    Factor first. Only now is x + 5 a factor rather than a term.
  2. x − 5
    Valid for x ≠ −5, since the original was undefined there.
  • Watch out
  • x + 5x is not 5. The x on top is a term, so it does not cancel.
  • Test any cancellation with a number. At x = 1, 1 + 51 = 6, not 5.
  • Adding numerators and denominators separately is the single most expensive habit on this list.

Linear equations

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: Unknown on both sides · Linear equations with variables on both sidesWatch: With brackets · Linear equations with parenthesesRead: The method, written out · Multi-step equations reviewPractise: Unknown on both sides · Equations with variables on both sidesPractise: Unknown in a denominator · Rational equations intro
  1. Whatever you do, do it to both sides. That is the only rule; the rest is order.
  2. Clear brackets first. −(x − 4) = −x + 4.
  3. Then clear fractions by multiplying every term by the common denominator. Every term, including the ones on the right.
  4. Collect the unknown on one side and the numbers on the other.
  5. Divide by the coefficient of the unknown.
  6. If the unknown sits in a denominator, multiply through by it, then check. A value that makes any original denominator zero is not a solution.
Solve 2x + 13 − x − 42 = 1
  1. 2(2x + 1) − 3(x − 4) = 6
    Multiply every term by 6, the common denominator.
  2. 4x + 2 − 3x + 12 = 6
    Expand. The minus reaches both terms of the second bracket.
  3. x + 14 = 6
    Collect.
  4. x = −8
    Check: −153 − −122 = −5 + 6 = 1.
  • Watch out
  • Multiplying by the common denominator and forgetting the right-hand side.
  • −3(x − 4) is −3x + 12. Both signs change.
  • With the unknown in a denominator, an answer that makes it zero must be thrown away.

Quadratic equations

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: Solving quadratics by factoringWatch: The formula · The quadratic formulaRead: The method, written out · Solving quadratics by factoring reviewPractise: Quadratics by factoringPractise: Using the formula · Quadratic formula
  1. Get everything to one side. ax2 + bx + c = 0,a ≠ 0
  2. Read off a, b and c with their signs. The signs are where the marks go.
  3. Try factoring. If it factors, it is faster. A product is zero only when one of its factors is zero.
  4. Otherwise use the formula. x = −b ± b2 − 4ac2a
  5. The discriminant answers how many roots before you find them. Δ = b2 − 4acΔ > 0: twoΔ = 0: oneΔ < 0: none in ℝ
Solve 2x2 − 7x + 3 = 0
  1. a = 2,  b = −7,  c = 3
    Read them off with their signs.
  2. Δ = 49 − 24 = 25
    Positive, so there are two roots, and 25 is a square so they are tidy.
  3. x = 7 ± 54
    Put it in the formula.
  4. x = 3  or  x = 12
  • Watch out
  • b = −7 gives b2 = 49, positive. Squaring kills the sign.
  • (x − 2)(x + 5) = 4 tells you nothing. Only a product equal to zero splits.
  • A negative Δ means no real solution, which in a business question usually means the target is never reached.

  3. For 5x2 − 2x − 7 = 0, what is Δ?

Turning a situation into an equation

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: A situation into an equation · Linear equation word problemsPractise: Writing the equation · Writing linear equations word problemsPractise: Writing and solving · Linear equations word problemsPractise: Two-step problems · Two-step equations word problems
  1. Name the unknown, with its unit. Write it down. "Let s = the number of soups sold."
  2. Write every other quantity in terms of it. If 80 items sold and s are soups, the rest is 80 − s.
  3. Find the sentence that says two things are equal. That is the equation.
  4. Solve it. Now it is only algebra.
  5. Answer in words, with the unit, and check it against the situation. A negative count or a price of 0.003 means the setup was wrong, not the arithmetic.
A stall sells rolls at 4.50 and soups at 7.00. It sold 80 items and took 440.00. How many soups?
  1. Let s = soups, so rolls = 80 − s
    One unknown is enough; everything else follows from it.
  2. 7s + 4.5(80 − s) = 440
    The total takings is the sentence that says two things are equal.
  3. 2.5s + 360 = 440
    Expand and collect.
  4. s = 32
    32 soups and 48 rolls. Check: 32(7) + 48(4.5) = 224 + 216 = 440.
  • Watch out
  • Starting to write algebra before defining the letter. Half the lost marks are here.
  • Mixing units. If one price is in francs, all of them are.
  • Answering with a bare number. The question asked how many soups.

    3. The weekly income of 7 200 CHF
    4. The weekly surplus of 2 300 CHF
  3. With V(t) = 50000 + 2300t in francs, what is the value of the business after 26 weeks?
  4. At the break-even point, which two quantities are set equal to each other? Write the equation.
  5. Rivella Coffee sells roasted beans at 24 CHF a kilo. Each kilo costs 9 CHF in green beans and packaging, and the roastery costs 18 000 CHF a month to run whatever it produces. How many kilos a month must it sell to break even?

Inequalities and percentages

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: Multi-step inequalitiesWatch: Percentages · Percent problemsRead: Double inequalities · Compound inequalities reviewPractise: Multi-step linear inequalitiesPractise: Percentages · Percent word problems
  1. An inequality solves like an equation, with one exception.
  2. Multiplying or dividing by a negative flips the sign. −2x > 6⇒x < −3
  3. A double inequality: do the same thing to all three parts. 2 ≤ 3x − 1 ≤ 8⇒1 ≤ x ≤ 3
  4. A percentage is a multiplier. That is the whole idea. p% of A = p100 · A
  5. Up by p% multiplies; down by p% multiplies too. ×(1 + p100)×(1 − p100)
  6. Successive changes multiply. They never add.
A price rises 20%, then falls 20%. What is the net change?
  1. ×1.20  then  ×0.80
    Each change is a multiplier on what came before.
  2. 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96
    Multiply the multipliers.
  3. a 4% fall
    Not zero. The fall is taken on the larger price.
  • Watch out
  • −2x > 6 gives x < −3. Dividing by the negative flips it.
  • Up 20% then down 20% is not back where you started.
  • A percentage point is not a percent. From 4% to 6% is two points and a 50% rise.

  3. Solve |x − 2| = 5.

    2. x = 7 only
    3. x = 3 or x = −3
    4. x = −7 or x = 3
  4. Solve |2x − 1| < 7.

Systems of linear equations

Not started

Khan AcademyWatch: By substitution · Solving systems of equations with substitutionPractise: By substitution · Systems of equations with substitutionPractise: By elimination · Systems of equations with elimination
  1. Two unknowns need two independent equations. Independent means neither is a multiple of the other.
  2. Substitution: make one equation say x = something, then put that into the other. The other one. Putting it back into the equation you rearranged gives 0 = 0.
  3. Elimination: scale one or both so a letter cancels when you add or subtract.
  4. If the letters vanish and a false statement is left, there is no solution. 0 = 5. The lines are parallel.
  5. If the letters vanish and a true statement is left, there are infinitely many. 0 = 0. It was the same line twice.
  6. Check the pair in both equations. One equation cannot catch an error.
Solve 3x + 2y = 19 and x − y = 3
  1. x = y + 3
    Rearrange the simpler of the two.
  2. 3(y + 3) + 2y = 19
    Substitute into the other equation.
  3. 5y = 10,  y = 2
    Expand and collect.
  4. x = 5,  y = 2
    Check both: 15 + 4 = 19 and 5 − 2 = 3.
  • Watch out
  • Substituting back into the equation you just rearranged. It always gives 0 = 0.
  • Subtracting equations and forgetting that the signs on the right change too.
  • Checking in one equation only. A slip survives that.

Practice  ·  Systems of linear equations

  1. Which start is the quicker one for y = 3x − 5 and 2x + 4y = 8?
    1. Substitution, because y is already alone in the first equation.
    2. Elimination, because the coefficients of x are already opposite.
    3. Elimination, after multiplying the first equation by 4.
    4. Neither. A system in which one unknown is already isolated has no solution.
  2. Solve 3x + 2y = 16 and x − y = 2. Give the value of x.
  3. Solve 2x + 3y = 19 and 4x − 3y = 11 by elimination. Give the value of y.
  4. Solve 2x + 3y = 7 and 4x + 6y = 5.
    1. There is no solution. The unknowns vanish and leave 0 = 9, which is false, so the lines are parallel and distinct.
    2. There are infinitely many solutions. The unknowns vanish, so the two equations are the same line.
    3. x = 2 and y = 1
    4. There is one solution, but elimination cannot find it.
  5. Solve x − 2y = 3 and 3x − 6y = 9.
    1. There are infinitely many solutions: the second equation is three times the first, so every pair with x = 3 + 2y works.
    2. There is no solution, because both unknowns vanish.
    3. x = 3 and y = 0 is the only solution.
    4. There is one solution, at the origin.
  6. A student offers (x, y) = (5, 1) for the system 2x + y = 11 and 3x − 2y = 6. Is it a solution?
    1. No. It satisfies the first equation but not the second. The solution is (4, 3).
    2. Yes. It satisfies the first equation, and one equation is enough to fix a pair.
    3. No. It satisfies neither equation.
    4. Yes, provided the system has infinitely many solutions.
